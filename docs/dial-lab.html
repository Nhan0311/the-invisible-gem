Build Your Own Dial

The same projection the thesis runs on Grenoble, as a tool: choose a latitude and a surface, and read the hour lines off the sphere.

Chapter II rebuilt Father Bonfa's fresco by projecting an ideal celestial sphere at Grenoble's latitude onto the staircase. The construction is not special to Grenoble: it works for any latitude and any flat surface. Set the dial below and watch the hour lines fall exactly where the planes of light cut the plane.

iWhat you are looking at

Everything is one construction, straight out of Chapter II §5. The polar axis tilts φ above the northern horizon — the style of the dial points along it. Then:

  • French hours — planes through the polar axis, rotated in steps of 15° from the noon meridian. Where each plane cuts the surface is an hour line.
  • Babylonian / Italian hours — the horizon plane itself, rotated about the polar axis in 15° steps.
  • Zodiac / month arcs — cones about the polar axis at declinations 0°, ±11.5°, ±20°, ±23°27′; the shadow of the style's tip runs along one of them for a fortnight at a time.
  • Celestial houses — the same as French hours but stepped 30°, one house per two solar hours.

Switch the surface to Reflected and a mirror on a window sill throws the spot onto the ceiling; the faint band is where that spot travels between the two solstices — the same figure the thesis draws for the Grenoble stair (Fig. 2.5, 2.40).

The exports are real geometry: .OBJ opens the line network in any 3-D tool, .SVG is the flat dial face for the horizontal or vertical surface, ready to scale and scribe.

Built with three.js. Declination uses a first-order approximation and the equation of time is ignored, so read it as a construction aid, not an ephemeris.
